A home inspection is one of the smartest moves you'll make when buying in the Triad, and one of the most misunderstood. Some buyers skip it to win a competitive offer. Others get one and have no idea what to do with the report. Both are mistakes.

Here's how a home inspection actually works in North Carolina, with no scare tactics.

## What is a home inspection in North Carolina?

A home inspection is a top-to-bottom visual check of the house by a licensed inspector you hire before you're locked into the purchase.

The inspector looks at the roof, foundation and structure, crawlspace or basement, HVAC, electrical panel and wiring, plumbing, water heater, attic, windows, and overall condition. Around here you also want eyes on crawlspace moisture, signs of past water intrusion, and the well and septic if the house has them. You get a written report, usually with photos.

The inspector's job is to inform you, not to pass or fail the house. No house is perfect, not even a new one.

## Do you have to get a home inspection in NC?

No, it isn't legally required. Skipping one is still a gamble.

This isn't legal advice, just how it works in real life. North Carolina is a buyer-beware state, so much of the responsibility to investigate falls on you. Sellers can also answer "No Representation" on many disclosure items, which means they aren't telling you one way or the other. The inspection is how you find out for yourself. My [NC buyer-beware guide](/guides/north-carolina-buyer-beware/) explains this in more detail.

## How does the inspection fit into due diligence?

You schedule the inspection during the due diligence period. That's the window in the contract when you can investigate the property and walk away for almost any reason.

You usually pay a [due diligence fee](/guides/due-diligence-fees-nc/) up front, and during that period the ball is in your court. Once it ends, your options narrow fast. So book the inspector early, ideally the day you go under contract. You want time to read the report, get contractor quotes, and respond, not a panic at the buzzer.

## Can you ask the seller to fix things after the inspection?

Yes, you can ask. But in North Carolina the seller isn't automatically required to fix anything. It becomes a negotiation, and you have a few realistic paths:

- Ask the seller to make repairs before closing.
- Ask for a price reduction or a credit and handle repairs yourself.
- Accept the house as-is if the issues are minor.
- Walk away during due diligence if the inspection finds something serious, like structural problems, a failing roof, or septic trouble.

The point isn't to nitpick every scuff. It's to catch the things that would change your decision or your budget. A good local agent helps you tell a small fix from a real problem.

## Does new construction need an inspection?

Yes, just as much as an older home. Builders are human and crews move fast. Missed HVAC work, unfinished plumbing connections, and grading or drainage problems happen.

With all the new construction around Lexington, Thomasville, and Davidson County, this comes up constantly. An independent inspector, one you hire rather than the builder's crew, catches those items while the builder is still on the hook. See my guide to [new construction near Lexington](/guides/new-construction-near-lexington-nc/).

## What about homes with a well and septic?

In rural Davidson County, plan on having the septic and well checked specifically, sometimes by a specialist in addition to the general inspector. A septic system permitted for fewer bedrooms than the house is being used for, or a well with water-quality issues, is exactly what you want to know before closing. Read [buying a house with well and septic in Davidson County](/guides/buying-well-septic-davidson-county/).

## Should you still inspect an as-is home?

Usually, yes. "As-is" means the seller won't make repairs. It doesn't mean you shouldn't know what you're buying. The inspection tells you whether the price makes sense or whether you should walk away during due diligence.
