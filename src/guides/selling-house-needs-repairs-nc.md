A house that needs repairs still deserves a clear selling plan. Start by finding out which work changes the decision, which work can wait, and what each option could leave you with after costs.

## Do you have to repair everything before listing?

Compare the work with its expected effect on the sale rather than assuming every repair is necessary. Get written estimates and discuss financing or condition issues that could affect buyers. Cosmetic preferences and unresolved system problems need different treatment.

## What does listing as is require you to think through?

Think through condition information, buyer expectations, access for inspections, and realistic pricing. Have your agent and attorney explain disclosure and contract obligations. An as-is description is not a substitute for that conversation.

## How should you compare an as-is offer with a listing?

Compare written terms and estimated net proceeds after costs, repairs, and timing. Review contingencies, closing obligations, and any assignment language with an attorney. A simpler-looking offer still needs a close read.

## What should you do first?

Gather repair history, known concerns, and available estimates. Then discuss two or three practical sale scenarios before spending. Avoid a renovation project driven only by guessing what an unknown buyer will want.

## Compare repair and as-is outcomes

Estimate likely sale proceeds after preparation, then subtract repairs, carrying costs, concessions, and selling expenses. Compare that with an as-is path based on its actual net terms and contingencies. A faster sale may offer convenience but a different net return. ## Three realistic ways to sell a house needing work

**List in its current condition:** Set expectations through accurate condition information, property-specific pricing, and appropriate disclosures. Buyers may still ask for inspections or financing-related repairs.

**Make selected repairs first:** Consider work that improves safety, prevents further damage, or helps an otherwise qualified buyer finance the home. Obtain written quotes and include the time and holding costs in your plan. Avoid assuming every dollar invested is recovered.

**Consider a direct as-is offer:** Compare written purchase price, earnest money, inspection or cancellation rights, buyer funding, closing date, and estimated seller net proceeds. Confirm the contract's details before treating an offer as certain.

## Which problems can affect buyer financing?

Visible defects, deferred maintenance, safety concerns, and major system issues can influence inspections, appraisals, insurance, and loan eligibility. The requirements depend on the lender and loan product; never assume a specific home automatically qualifies for FHA, VA, USDA, or conventional financing.

## Compare net proceeds before choosing a route

For each option, estimate **sale price minus repair expense, selling and closing costs, concessions, and carrying costs**. The fastest offer is not always the highest-net offer, and the highest price isn't necessarily the lowest-risk path.
