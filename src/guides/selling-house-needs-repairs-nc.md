A house that needs repairs still deserves a clear selling plan. Start by finding out which work changes the decision, which work can wait, and what each option could leave you with after costs.

## Do you have to repair everything before listing?

Compare the work with its expected effect on the sale rather than assuming every repair is necessary. Get written estimates and discuss financing or condition issues that could affect buyers. Cosmetic preferences and unresolved system problems need different treatment.

## What does listing as is require you to think through?

Think through condition information, buyer expectations, access for inspections, and realistic pricing. Have your agent and attorney explain disclosure and contract obligations. An as-is description is not a substitute for that conversation.

## How should you compare an as-is offer with a listing?

Compare written terms and estimated net proceeds after costs, repairs, and timing. Review contingencies, closing obligations, and any assignment language with an attorney. A simpler-looking offer still needs a close read.

## What should you do first?

Gather repair history, known concerns, and available estimates. Then discuss two or three practical sale scenarios before spending. Avoid a renovation project driven only by guessing what an unknown buyer will want.

## Compare repair and as-is outcomes

Estimate likely sale proceeds after preparation, then subtract repairs, carrying costs, concessions, and selling expenses. Compare that with an as-is path based on its actual net terms and contingencies. A faster sale may offer convenience but a different net return.
