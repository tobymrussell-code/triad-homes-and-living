The best improvement before selling is not automatically the one with the biggest national ROI percentage. It is the work that solves a real problem, fits the house and leaves you with more money after the project, delay and risk.

**The practical order is simple:** clear and clean first, repair obvious problems second, make selective cosmetic updates third, and price major renovations before you commit.

## Which low-cost preparation should come first?

These jobs often matter because they remove buyer objections and make the home's condition easier to understand. No credible national source provides a universal resale ROI for each small task, so the ranges below are **planning bands**, not value promises. Get local quotes.

| Improvement | Planning cost band | Effort | When it makes sense | When to skip or limit it |
| --- | ---: | --- | --- | --- |
| Declutter and edit rooms | $0–$500 | Low–medium | Rooms feel crowded or buyers cannot see storage and layout | Do not pay to store items with no plan for them after the move |
| Junk removal / cleanout | $200–$2,000+ | Medium | Debris, old furniture or outbuilding contents distract from the property | A deep estate or hoarding cleanout needs separate quotes, safety planning and more time |
| Deep cleaning | $250–$900+ | Low | Almost every occupied or recently vacated home | Do not clean around an active leak, mold-like growth or unsafe material—address the cause first |
| Yard cleanup and mulch | $300–$2,500+ | Medium | Overgrowth hides the house or makes maintenance look deferred | Skip elaborate landscaping buyers may not value or want to maintain |
| Small repairs and touch-ups | $200–$2,500+ | Low–medium | Loose hardware, damaged trim, holes, sticking doors or obvious incomplete work | Avoid endless punch-list work with no visible or functional payoff |
| Light fixtures and bulbs | $150–$2,000+ | Low–medium | Rooms are dim, fixtures are damaged or finishes are distracting | Keep good fixtures that fit the home; electrical work belongs with a qualified professional |

**My rule:** if buyers will notice the problem in the first five minutes, put it near the top of the list. If the work is mainly personal preference, make it earn its place with real comparable-sale evidence.

## Which cosmetic improvements are often worth pricing?

| Improvement | Planning cost band | Effort | When it makes sense | When to skip or limit it |
| --- | ---: | --- | --- | --- |
| Targeted interior paint | $400–$4,500+ | Medium | Walls are damaged, patched, very dark or inconsistent | Do not repaint neutral rooms that already present well |
| Flooring repair or replacement | $500–$10,000+ | Medium–high | Flooring is damaged, unsafe, strongly mismatched or creates a major first impression problem | Whole-house replacement can be expensive; cleaning or targeted repair may be enough |
| Cabinet, hardware and faucet refresh | $300–$6,000+ | Medium | Kitchen or bath functions well but looks tired | Do not disguise failing cabinets, water damage or layout problems with surface-only work |
| Countertops | $2,000–$7,500+ | Medium | Existing surfaces are damaged and competing homes consistently have an updated finish | Skip premium stone that moves the room far beyond the home's price point |
| Exterior touch-up / front entry | $250–$4,000+ | Medium | Peeling trim, a worn door or weak entry is hurting the first impression | A full door or exterior replacement only makes sense when condition and local competition support it |
| Basic staging | $0–$3,000+ | Low–medium | Furniture placement, scale or vacant rooms make the layout hard to read | Do not rent expensive furniture for rooms that already photograph and show clearly |

## When do systems and major repairs make sense?

Roofs, HVAC, plumbing, electrical, moisture and structural concerns do not behave like decorative upgrades. They may affect financing, insurance, inspection negotiations and buyer confidence. The decision is not always “replace it” or “ignore it.” Sometimes the best move is to diagnose the issue, document its condition, get written options and price the house accordingly.

| Project | Typical decision | When doing it may make sense | When an as-is or credit strategy may be better |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Roof | Repair, replace or document remaining life | Active leaks, missing material, insurability or financing concerns | Replacement cost is unlikely to be recovered and buyers can obtain coverage |
| HVAC | Service, repair or replace | Non-working, unsafe, very near failure or creating a financing issue | System works and price already reflects age |
| Moisture / crawl space | Diagnose source before choosing scope | Active water entry, damaged materials or conditions buyers cannot reasonably evaluate | Never cover or cosmetically hide the issue; disclose and price with professional input |
| Electrical / plumbing | Correct safety and functional defects | Known hazards, failed components, leaks or unpermitted work need resolution | Broad modernization with no buyer or financing requirement may not pay back |
| Windows | Repair, replace selected units or leave | Rot, failed units or water intrusion are material problems | Replacing every functional window solely for resale may not recover the cost |

Get qualified inspections and contractor quotes where needed. This guide is not a substitute for engineering, environmental, legal, tax, appraisal or contracting advice.

## When should you skip a major remodel?

Be very careful when the project:

- pushes the home well above the finish level of nearby competition;
- uses high-end materials buyers in the price range are unlikely to pay for;
- changes a functional layout mainly to suit your taste;
- depends on a best-case sale price to break even;
- needs permits, long lead times or several trades when your sale timeline is short;
- consumes cash you need for the move or creates costly financing;
- could uncover hidden work after demolition;
- makes the house the most improved property on the street without supporting sales.

Zonda's defined 2025 national **minor kitchen remodel** recovered 112.9% of cost, while its **major midrange kitchen remodel** recovered 50.9%. That does not mean every small kitchen project pays or that no major renovation works. It shows why scope matters.

## How do you calculate the real renovation ROI?

Do not call the entire post-improvement sale price the “return.” Start with the value the house likely has today.

**Incremental value created = estimated post-improvement value − estimated as-is value**

**Expected net gain = incremental value created − project cost − carrying/time cost − selling-cost impact**

**ROI = expected net gain ÷ total improvement-related cost × 100**

Example: if a home is worth an estimated $300,000 as-is, could be worth $325,000 after the work, and the all-in project cost is $22,000, the estimated net gain is $3,000—not $25,000.

## How should a Triad seller use these numbers?

Build three scenarios for the same house:

| Sale path | Costs to include | Evidence to compare |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Sell in current condition | Basic preparation, likely concessions, holding and selling costs | Similar dated or fixer sales and current competition |
| Targeted preparation | Written quotes, contingency, delay, financing and selling costs | Sales with genuinely comparable updates |
| Larger renovation | Full scope, permits, contingency, carrying costs and risk | Sales with similar renovation quality, location, lot and utility setup |

In the Triad, one countywide percentage cannot account for different towns, rural versus in-town utilities, price points, lot types, buyer pools or contractor availability. A CMA should separate closed sales, pending competition and active listings, then explain which updated homes are actually comparable.

## What if repairs are not worth doing?

You still have choices. You can list in present condition with realistic expectations, complete only safety or financing-related work, offer a negotiated credit when appropriate, or compare a direct investor/as-is option. The useful comparison is **estimated net, time, certainty and risk**—not just the highest headline price.

## What should you do before hiring a contractor?

1. Get a property-specific value range for the as-is and improved condition.
2. Define the exact scope. “Update the kitchen” is not a usable bid.
3. Get written quotes and a realistic schedule.
4. Add a contingency for surprises and delays.
5. Confirm permits and qualified trades where required.
6. Run the worksheet using conservative value and cost assumptions.
7. Decide whether the likely net gain is worth the cash, disruption and risk.
