A useful price comes from the homes a buyer could compare with yours today. Start with relevant evidence and your property’s condition, then connect the asking price to a selling plan.

## Which comparisons should matter?

Use relevant current and closed property comparisons with attention to location, condition, size, and features. Ask why each comparison belongs in the analysis. A county-wide average can hide the details that make your home different.

## How does condition affect the plan?

Evaluate known work and buyer questions before choosing a price. Compare written repair estimates with pricing scenarios. Avoid assuming a buyer will overlook an expensive unknown because the paint is fresh.

## Should you price around the amount you need?

Understand your estimated net proceeds, then compare that goal with the evidence buyers will use. Ask about payoff and closing costs through appropriate professionals. Your desired net is a planning question, not proof of market value.

## What should you review after listing?

Review actual showing feedback, relevant competition, and changes in your property’s position. Discuss what the evidence supports before adjusting price or preparation. A plan should have review points without promising a specific result.

## Compare the right homes, not just nearby listings

Choose recent closed sales and current competition that attract a similar buyer. Evaluate square footage, renovation quality, lot usefulness, property condition, and service availability. A home outside Lexington city limits may have different ownership costs from one downtown. Review showing feedback and the competitive landscape after listing rather than anchoring solely to the seller's target net proceeds.

## What goes into a comparative market analysis?

A useful comparative market analysis (CMA) separates **closed sales**, **pending sales**, and **active listings**. Closed sales show what buyers actually paid; pending sales show activity but often not the final terms; active listings represent current competition, not proven values. Ask why each home is comparable: location, lot, heated living area, condition, renovations, and utility setup all matter.

For Thomasville or Lexington, avoid relying on the ZIP code alone. Compare parcel location and municipal or county services. A renovated house, a dated house, and a home needing foundation work may attract different buyers even if their square footage is similar.

## What can a seller control before listing?

You can influence presentation, access for showings, documented repairs, and the initial asking price. You cannot control interest rates, competing inventory, appraisal conclusions, or the buyer's financing. Build a plan that includes photographs, accurate disclosures, key property records, and a date for reviewing feedback.

## What does the seller actually keep?

Ask for a **seller net sheet** that shows a realistic price range, estimated mortgage payoff, applicable brokerage compensation, negotiated concessions, prorations, taxes, repairs, and other closing expenses. Compare several scenarios instead of a single optimistic figure. The net sheet is an estimate, not a guarantee.

## Why do price brackets matter so much?

Buyers search online inside price ranges, often in round numbers. A house listed just above a common cutoff can disappear from searches capped at that number. Before you pick a price, look at the brackets your likely buyer is searching and where your home falls inside them.

## Why do the first two weeks matter?

A new listing gets the most attention in its first couple of weeks. If showings are slow or nobody's making offers in that window, the market is telling you something about price, condition, or presentation. Waiting it out usually costs more than adjusting early.

## Should you price high to leave room to negotiate?

Usually not. Overpricing tends to bring fewer showings, more days on market, and price cuts that make buyers wonder what's wrong. A home that sits often ends up selling for less than a well-priced listing would have. That's the hidden cost of starting too high.

## Why do some Triad homes sell fast while others sit?

Homes that move tend to be priced for their condition, look good online, and are easy to show. Homes that sit are usually priced against the seller's hopes instead of today's competition, or have condition issues buyers didn't expect. Your real competition is the handful of homes a buyer will compare yours against this week, not every sale in the county.
