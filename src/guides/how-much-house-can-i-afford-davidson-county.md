"How much house can I afford?" is really two questions in one coat, and most buyers only ask the first one. What a lender will approve and what you can comfortably live with are different numbers.

I help buyers across Davidson County, including Thomasville, Lexington, Welcome, and Wallburg, plus High Point and Winston-Salem. The most common problem I see isn't overspending. It's people who don't know their real number, so they either shop below what they could afford or fall for a house the bank was never going to approve.

## What's the difference between affordable and approved?

A lender tells you the most they'll let you borrow. What you can afford is the payment that still covers your car, childcare, weekends, and a surprise water heater. The bank's maximum is a ceiling, not a target.

A long-standing rule of thumb is the **28/36 rule**: keep your total housing payment around 28% of gross monthly income, and all debt payments combined, including car, student loans, and credit cards, under about 36%. Plenty of solid buyers stretch past it on purpose, but it's a sane starting line, and lenders run similar math in the background.

## How do you calculate your Triad home budget?

Start with four numbers, in this order:

1. **Your income.** It sets the top of the range.
2. **Your monthly debts.** Each car payment is money a lender won't count toward a mortgage.
3. **Your down payment.** A bigger one lowers the loan. On most conventional loans, 20% down also removes private mortgage insurance.
4. **The carrying costs on that specific house.** Property taxes, homeowners insurance, and mortgage insurance are bundled into your payment, so two houses at the same price don't always cost the same to own.

That last number matters more here than people expect. Davidson, Guilford, and Forsyth counties each set their own property tax rate, and homes inside city limits pay a municipal rate on top of the county rate. A house just outside Thomasville city limits can have a different tax bill than a nearly identical one a mile away. Run the actual taxes on the address before you fall for it.

## How much income do you need to buy in Davidson County?

Think in multiples instead of a magic salary. A common starting point is a home priced around three to four times your gross annual income. Go lower if you carry a lot of other debt, higher with a big down payment and no car loans. As a quick example, three to four times a $70,000 income works out to roughly $210,000 to $280,000. Your rate, debts, and down payment can move that either way.

I won't give you a single price because interest rates move, and a payment that works at one rate doesn't work at another. Davidson County is still one of the more attainable corners of the Triad, which is why so many people working in Lexington, Winston-Salem, or High Point end up here.

## Does where you buy change what you can afford?

Yes, a lot. Your budget doesn't just pick a price, it picks a map. The same money that buys a smaller place near downtown Winston-Salem might buy a bigger house with a yard in Welcome or rural Davidson County, with a longer commute as the trade.

Loan programs matter too. Some rural parts of the county may qualify for USDA financing. Read [USDA loans in Davidson County](/guides/usda-loans-davidson-county-nc/). Builders sometimes offer incentives toward closing costs or a lower rate, but those come and go, so ask what's actually available.

## What costs do buyers forget?

The monthly payment isn't the whole story. Plan for:

- **The due diligence fee and earnest money** when you go under contract. The due diligence fee goes to the seller and is generally not refundable.
- **Closing costs.** See [closing costs in North Carolina](/guides/closing-costs-north-carolina/).
- **The home inspection,** plus well and septic checks on many rural homes. The septic permit controls how many bedrooms a house can legally be marketed with, no matter how the rooms are used.

Also remember that North Carolina is a buyer-beware state, and you'll sign a buyer agency agreement before touring. That isn't legal advice, just how it works.
