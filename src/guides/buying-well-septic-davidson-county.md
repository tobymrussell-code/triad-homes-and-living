A lot of the more private places in Davidson County, out in Welcome, Wallburg, Tyro, Reeds, Denton, and the rural stretches around Lexington and Thomasville, run on a private well and septic system. None of that is scary. It just doesn't show up in the listing photos, and if you're coming from a place where every house was on city water and sewer, it can catch you off guard.

Here's what I actually check for my buyers before they write an offer on a well and septic home.

## What does well and septic mean for a buyer?

The home handles its own water and wastewater instead of connecting to a city system. A private well pulls drinking water from the ground, and a septic system treats wastewater on your own property. In rural Davidson County that's completely normal.

It just adds two big questions to your checklist: Is the water good and reliable? Is the septic system the right size and in working order for this house?

## How do you know how many bedrooms the septic is approved for?

This is the single most important thing. **The septic permit, not the listing, decides how many bedrooms a home can legally have.** A system is sized and permitted for a specific number of bedrooms, because that's how the county estimates wastewater.

Here's where buyers get burned. A listing might say four bedrooms, but if the septic is only permitted for three, the fourth room is really a bonus room, office, or flex space. That affects value, financing, and resale. Septic records are handled through Davidson County Environmental Health, and pulling the permit to confirm the bedroom count is a normal part of your homework.

## What should you check on the well?

Get the water tested and confirm the well produces enough. The two things to confirm are:

- **Water quality,** through a lab test for the common issues.
- **Flow or yield,** meaning whether it keeps up with a full household.

Also find out where the well is, how old it is, and whether it's shared with a neighbor. A shared well comes with a shared agreement you'll want to read.

## How do you know the septic system works?

Have it inspected, and ideally pumped and looked at, before you close. A septic inspection is separate from your regular home inspection, and it's worth it. The inspector looks at the tank, the drain field, and whether the system handles the load without backing up or surfacing. Ask the seller for any records, and check what the county has on file.

Your home inspector will flag plenty in the house itself, but the septic usually needs its own specialist. See my [home inspection guide](/guides/home-inspection-north-carolina/) for how the two fit together.

## Where does this fit in the North Carolina buying process?

All of it happens during your due diligence period, the window after you're under contract when you investigate the property.

This isn't legal advice, but here's how it works in real life. North Carolina is a buyer-beware state, so it's on you and your agent to check this out. Sellers can answer "No Representation" on parts of the disclosure, so you can't rely on it alone. Due diligence is your chance to pull the permit, test the well, inspect the septic, and still negotiate or walk away if something's off. My [NC buyer-beware guide](/guides/north-carolina-buyer-beware/) explains more.

## Is a well and septic home worth buying?

For a lot of buyers, absolutely. You usually get more land, more privacy, no monthly water and sewer bill, and real country quiet. The tradeoff is that you own the system, so maintenance is on you. The tank needs pumping on a regular schedule, and the well needs occasional attention.

That isn't a dealbreaker. It's the honest other side of the coin, and plenty of my happiest buyers in rural Davidson County would tell you the space is worth it. If you're the seller, read [selling a house with well and septic](/guides/selling-well-septic-davidson-county/).
