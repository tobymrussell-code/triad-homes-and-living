HOAs trip up more buyers than almost anything else, especially people relocating to the Triad or buying new construction around Thomasville, Lexington, and Davidson County. An HOA isn't automatically good or bad. Sometimes it keeps a neighborhood sharp and protects your value. Sometimes it's a monthly bill plus a rulebook you didn't read closely enough. The difference is a little homework before you fall for the house.

## What is an HOA, and what does it control?

A homeowners association manages a neighborhood's shared rules, common areas, and dues. When you buy, you agree to follow its standards.

What an HOA can and can't do is spelled out in its governing documents: the declaration (often called CC&Rs), bylaws, architectural guidelines, and board-adopted rules. Many North Carolina communities also fall under the **North Carolina Planned Community Act (Chapter 47F)**, which sets baseline rules. Those documents usually cover exterior changes, landscaping, parking and vehicles, trash, pets, noise, common areas, and even flags and signs.

You'll find HOAs across the Triad, especially in newer subdivisions and townhome communities around High Point, Winston-Salem, Kernersville, and newer parts of Davidson County. They're much rarer out in rural Welcome, Wallburg, and the back roads.

## How much are HOA dues, and can they go up?

Dues fund shared expenses, and yes, they can go up. The HOA can also charge **special assessments** for big-ticket items.

Regular dues cover day-to-day costs like common-area maintenance, landscaping, amenities, insurance on shared property, and reserves for future repairs. The amount varies a lot. A townhome HOA that handles exterior upkeep and a pool costs more than a subdivision that just mows the entrance.

Special assessments are what surprise people. If a shared roof fails or private roads need repaving and reserves are short, every owner can get an extra bill. Look at the budget and reserves before you buy. Healthy reserves are a green flag. A broke HOA that keeps putting off maintenance is a yellow one.

## What should you check before buying?

Here's the checklist I run for buyers around Davidson County and the Triad:

- **Governing documents.** The declaration, bylaws, architectural guidelines, and current rules. This is where the real story lives.
- **Dues and what they include.** The amount, how often they're billed, and exactly what they cover.
- **Budget and reserves.** North Carolina associations generally have to make financial records available to owners.
- **Special assessments.** Past history, and anything pending. Ask directly.
- **The rules you'd actually bump into.** Pets, rentals, short-term rentals like Airbnb, parking, fences, sheds, and exterior colors.
- **How fines work.** Under the Planned Community Act, an HOA generally must give notice and a hearing before fining you, and a fine can't exceed $100 per violation. A continuing violation can be fined daily.

This isn't legal advice. If something in the documents worries you, a real estate attorney can review the fine print with you.

## Does the seller have to tell you about the HOA?

In most North Carolina residential sales, the seller provides the state's Residential Property and Owners' Association Disclosure Statement, which has an owners' association section. Don't treat it as the whole picture.

North Carolina is a buyer-beware state, and sellers can answer "No Representation" on items. Use the disclosure as a starting point, then get the actual HOA documents. Your agent can usually request them from the HOA or its management company. My [NC buyer-beware guide](/guides/north-carolina-buyer-beware/) explains why that matters.

## Is an HOA good or bad?

Neither. It depends on you and the community. If you like tidy, uniform neighborhoods and don't mind rules, an HOA can be a real plus. If you want flexibility, plan to rent the home out, run a business from the garage, or pick your own paint color, a strict HOA can feel confining.

For relocation buyers, this is worth deciding early because it shapes which neighborhoods make sense.
