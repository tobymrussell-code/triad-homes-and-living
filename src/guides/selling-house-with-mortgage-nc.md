Short answer: yes, you can sell a house that still has a mortgage in North Carolina. Most sellers in Thomasville, Lexington, and Davidson County are doing exactly that. You almost never pay off the loan first. The payoff happens at the closing table, out of the money the buyer brings.

This trips people up more than it should. You pull up your mortgage balance, see a scary number, and think you can't sell until it's gone. That's not how it works.

## Do you have to pay off your mortgage before selling?

No. In almost every normal sale, your mortgage is paid off at closing using the buyer's funds.

Here's the flow. The buyer and their lender bring money to closing, which in North Carolina is handled by a real estate attorney. The attorney gets an official payoff from your lender, pays the loan in full from the sale proceeds, and sends any leftover money to you. The mortgage doesn't block the sale. It gets settled as part of it.

The only time you bring money to closing is when you owe more than the house nets.

## How do you know what you'll walk away with?

Your equity is roughly the sale price, minus what you owe, minus the costs of selling. That leftover number is what lands in your pocket, not the full sale price.

The attorney pays off the mortgage first. Then selling costs come out, such as attorney fees, the commission you agreed to, the state excise tax, property taxes owed up to the sale date, and anything you agreed to cover for the buyer. My [North Carolina closing costs guide](/guides/closing-costs-north-carolina/) breaks those down.

One thing most sellers miss: your payoff isn't the same as your last statement balance. It includes interest up to the day the loan is paid off and sometimes a small fee, so it's usually a little higher. If you want a real number before listing, I can put together a seller net sheet so you're not guessing.

## What if you owe more than the house is worth?

That's called being underwater, and you still have options. The worst move is assuming you're stuck and doing nothing.

If the gap is small, some sellers bring the difference to closing and move on. If it's large, a short sale may be possible, where the lender agrees to accept less than the full payoff. That's a longer, more involved process, and you shouldn't try it on your own. Either way, start by comparing an honest value for the house with your real payoff. Many Davidson County owners, especially those who've owned a while, have more equity than they think. My [Triad pricing guide](/guides/pricing-triad-home/) explains how a realistic price gets set.

## Does your loan type matter?

For most conventional, FHA, VA, and USDA loans, selling works the same way: the loan is paid off at closing and you keep the rest.

Almost all standard mortgages have a due-on-sale clause, which means the loan must be paid off when you sell rather than handed to the buyer. That's normal, and the closing attorney handles it. The rare exception is a truly assumable loan. If you think you have one, confirm it before you build plans around it.

## What if the house needs work, or you need to move fast?

The mortgage isn't the obstacle. Condition and timing are, and the payoff works the same either way.

You can list the house on the market, where the right buyer may pay more but you deal with repairs, showings, and timing. Or you can sell it as-is and trade some price for convenience.
