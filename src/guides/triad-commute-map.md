Before you save another house, map the places you have to reach from it. Your commute map should include the whole week, not just one drive to work.

## What should go on your commute map?

Include work, regular appointments, errands, and any other destinations you expect to repeat. Note which trips are daily and which are occasional. Those frequencies help you weigh a tradeoff honestly.

## Which starting point should you use?

Use the actual property’s driveway as the starting point. Check the route in both directions at relevant hours with current tools and a visit. Don’t use the town center or an agent’s generic drive-time claim.

## How do you compare more than one address?

Put candidate addresses in a simple table with the same destinations. Record routes to verify and any access concerns. Keep property condition and costs in separate columns so one advantage doesn’t hide another problem.

## What should you test in person?

Test the route out of the neighborhood, key turns, parking, and the trip home. Visit at times that match your schedule. A home can pass the online map check and still feel inconvenient in daily use.

## When should you narrow the search?

Narrow after the route check identifies a workable area and your property checklist confirms useful options. Keep more than one town if both work. The town name is a filter, not the finish line.

## Build a practical five-destination route test

Map work, groceries, appointments, regular family activities, and your most frequent recreational destination. Test each candidate home during normal travel times rather than relying on a single map estimate. Save the results alongside notes about home condition and carrying costs to compare tradeoffs across the Triad.
